# CLI Website Build Brief: *Reincarnation in “Crossing Brooklyn Ferry”*

## 1. Project Purpose

Build a creative, research-based website for a Walt Whitman and New York course. The site should reinterpret Walt Whitman’s **“Crossing Brooklyn Ferry”** as an archive of real people experiencing the East River crossing and its waterfront across three historical periods.

The main idea is **reincarnation as the recurrence of human experience**, not necessarily the literal rebirth of one soul. New people inhabit the same water, light, crowds, fatigue, departures, homecomings, chance encounters, and moments of beauty. The ferry itself also appears to undergo a life cycle: it is an ordinary necessity in Whitman’s century, declines and disappears from Fulton Ferry in the twentieth century, and returns in the twenty-first century as both public transit and urban spectacle.

This new documentary archive **replaces the earlier idea of three fictional characters**. Do not invent historical passengers, quotations, diary entries, reviews, or sources.

### Working title

**Reincarnation in “Crossing Brooklyn Ferry”: An Archive of Lives Across the East River**

### Working thesis

> “Crossing Brooklyn Ferry” imagines not the literal rebirth of one soul but the reincarnation of experience. Across new bodies and generations, riders encounter the same water, crowds, light, fatigue, chance meetings, and wonder. The ferry itself also dies and returns: its closure in 1924 and twenty-first-century revival turn Whitman’s address to future riders into material history.

### Important interpretive limit

Do not claim that Whitman was Buddhist, that he believed in reincarnation, or that the poem proves a specific Eastern religious doctrine unless reliable scholarship supports that claim. Use **reincarnation as a comparative interpretive metaphor**: lives change, but the experience of crossing returns.

## 3. Core Experience

The visitor follows a red thread through three “lives” of the crossing:

1. **First Life — The Everyday Crossing, 1850s–1890s**  
   The ferry is necessary, crowded, social, laborious, and sometimes unexpectedly beautiful.

2. **Death / Interlude — The Missing Ferry, 1900s–2000s**  
   Bridges and subways drain ferry traffic; Fulton Ferry service ends in 1924. The archive shifts from riders to people living beside an absent crossing: writers, workers, artists, tenants, and waterfront communities.

3. **Return — Commuter and Spectacle, 2000s–Present**  
   Ferry service returns. The crossing is simultaneously a commute, a local habit, a visitor attraction, a skyline view, and a rediscovery of the city.

The historical gap is not a problem to hide. Make the **absence of the ferry the conceptual center of the twentieth-century section**: a death interval between two lives.

## 4. Site Architecture

### A. Landing page: “What is it then between us?”

Include:

- the title and subtitle;
- a brief opening statement of the project’s argument;
- one short, verified line from “Crossing Brooklyn Ferry” addressing future readers/riders;
- an animated or illustrated red thread that travels across an aged-paper background;

The landing page should establish that Whitman sees people in the future occupying the same scene he occupies.

### B. Interpretive prologue: “Whitman’s future passengers”

Explain in about 150–200 words:

- the poem first appeared in 1856 as “Sun-Down Poem” and later became “Crossing Brooklyn Ferry”;
- Whitman describes an ordinary homeward commute while imagining unknown people who will cross after him;
- the website treats this recurrence as a form of reincarnation of experience;
- all archive voices are documentary rather than fictional.

### C. Main archive timeline

Create three large era panels. Each panel should contain:

- a short historical introduction;
- 3–5 archive cards;
- a period image or map when rights allow;
- one highlighted Whitman motif;
- a transition explaining what survives or changes in the next era.

Archive cards should be filterable by motif and media type. Recommended filters:

- **Mundane / Glamorous**
- **Work / Leisure**
- **Commute / Tourism**
- **Departure / Homecoming**
- **Crowd / Solitude**
- **Text / Image / Audio / Video**

### D. Ferry life-cycle interlude

Build a compact horizontal or vertical history timeline:

- **1814:** Robert Fulton’s steam ferry strengthens the Brooklyn–Manhattan connection.
- **1883:** Brooklyn Bridge opens and helps begin the long decline of ferry traffic.
- **1908:** subway service beneath the East River further disperses ferry crowds.
- **1924:** Fulton Ferry service ceases.
- **Mid-century:** the waterfront experiences industrial decline, neglect, and separation from ordinary public life.
- **1997:** the reconstructed Fulton Ferry Landing pier opens.
- **2006:** seasonal/water-taxi service returns near the historic site.
- **2011:** regular East River Ferry service begins.
- **2017:** the route becomes part of the citywide NYC Ferry system.

Verify the exact wording and dates against the linked historical sources before publication. If sources distinguish Fulton Ferry from other East River ferry routes, preserve that distinction.

### E. Supplementary map: “Whitman’s connection places”

The map should support the archive rather than dominate it. Suggested locations:

- Fulton Ferry Landing;
- the former Manhattan Fulton/Beekman ferry slips or South Street area;
- Brooklyn Bridge;
- Brooklyn Heights and the Promenade;
- DUMBO and Brooklyn Bridge Park;
- relevant modern NYC Ferry landing(s).

Clicking a place should reveal the voices, images, and events connected to it. If a map library or API would complicate static deployment, use a stylized illustrated map with accessible buttons instead of a live commercial map.

### F. Motif index: “The same experience in another body”

Create a grid or thread-network connecting accounts across centuries through recurring motifs:

- water and tide;
- sunset, fog, and changing light;
- crowds and strangers;
- work and commuting;
- fatigue and inconvenience;
- chance encounters;
- homecoming and departure;
- skyline and spectacle;
- absence and return.

Selecting a motif should reveal one account from each era, allowing the visitor to compare different lives without pretending they are the same person.

### G. Critical reflection

Add a 200–300 word concluding section that answers:

- What does the archive reveal that a conventional essay might not?
- Does the crossing still create the common humanity Whitman imagines?
- What changes when a necessary commute also becomes a tourist experience?
- Can a place “remember” people when the infrastructure disappears and returns?
- How does the site’s red thread visualize connection without erasing historical differences?

Include:

- retrieval dates for web posts and reviews;
- a short explanation of selection criteria;
- copyright/permission notes;
- a statement that excerpts have been shortened and that no voices were invented;

## 5. Archive Source Packet

Treat the following as **research leads to verify**, not text to copy wholesale. Use a short quotation only after checking it against the linked item. Prefer paraphrase plus a link for copyrighted material.

### Era I: Nineteenth century — “The Everyday Crossing”

#### 1. Walt Whitman, “Crossing Brooklyn Ferry”

- Date: first published 1856.
- Voice type: poem based on the familiar ferry commute.
- Role: the conceptual anchor, not the only nineteenth-century voice.
- Motifs: crowd, sunset, water, future riders, common experience.
- Source: [Walt Whitman Archive](https://whitmanarchive.org/item/ppp.01663_01750)

#### 2. Robert Bulkeley, travel diary, 1857

- Person: an eighteen-year-old Brooklyn clerk beginning a journey.
- Scene: leaves Livingston Street before dawn with a heavy valise, gets coffee, walks to Fulton Ferry, and crosses in darkness and fog that begins to clear.
- Why it belongs: an ordinary travel routine becomes cinematic through weather and light—the mundane and glamorous in one crossing.
- Use: paraphrase most of the passage; if quoted, keep the excerpt very short.
- Source: [1857 Diary of Robert Bulkeley, transcription by Ann Bradshaw](https://www.joycetice.com/diaries/1857bulk.htm)

#### 3. Thomas Butler Gunn, diary, September 29, 1851

- Person: writer and illustrator Thomas Butler Gunn.
- Scene: while crossing to Brooklyn, Gunn unexpectedly meets William Roberts on the ferryboat and goes home with him for conversation and a cigar.
- Why it belongs: the ferry is a social threshold where an ordinary trip becomes a chance encounter.
- Possible short excerpt to verify: “Met William Roberts on the Ferry-boat.”
- Rights: the manuscript image is presented as public-domain/open-access material; verify the item’s reuse statement.
- Source: [Thomas Butler Gunn diary page, Missouri History Museum/Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AThomas_Butler_Gunn_Diaries-_Volume_2%2C_page_191%2C_September_29-30%2C_1851.jpg)

#### 4. Jervis McEntee, diary, January 31, 1879

- Person: Hudson River School artist Jervis McEntee.
- Scene: companions join him at Fulton Ferry while he travels toward a funeral in Brooklyn.
- Why it belongs: a public crossing carries private grief and community obligation; it can connect the project’s themes of death, memory, and return without inventing supernatural claims.
- Verify the exact transcript before quoting.
- Source: [Smithsonian Archives of American Art: Jervis McEntee diary](https://www.aaa.si.edu/collection-features/diaries/jervis-mcentee/1879/January-31)

#### 5. Optional visual: “6, 7, 8, 9 o’clock on the Fulton Ferry,” ca. 1880

- Image: four male commuters from different apparent economic backgrounds.
- Why it belongs: shows class difference within the repeated daily rhythm of the crossing.
- Verify permission or educational-use conditions before reproducing.
- Source: [Brooklyn Public Library, Waterfront and Ferries research guide](https://www.bklynlibrary.org/cbh/collections/research-guides/waterfront-ferries)

#### Additional nineteenth-century image

- [Library of Congress: “The Fulton ferry boat,” 1890](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/97505116/)
- Confirm the item’s rights-and-access statement before downloading or displaying it.

### Era II: Twentieth century — “Death / The Missing Ferry”

This section should deliberately contain fewer active ferry riders. Its subject is what happens to a crossing after bridges, subways, and urban change interrupt the daily ritual Whitman knew.

#### 1. The final Fulton Ferry crossing, January 1924

- Find the original January 1924 newspaper coverage through Columbia, ProQuest Historical Newspapers, the Brooklyn Public Library, or another institutional archive.
- Look for a named passenger, ferry worker, ticket collector, or reporter’s observation from the final day.
- Do not quote the secondary blog as if it were the original eyewitness account.
- Research lead: [Ephemeral New York: “Old Fulton Ferry passes into history”](https://ephemeralnewyork.wordpress.com/2008/08/01/old-fulton-ferry-passes-into-history/)
- Supporting collection guide: [Brooklyn Public Library, Waterfront and Ferries](https://www.bklynlibrary.org/cbh/collections/research-guides/waterfront-ferries)

#### 2. Truman Capote, *A House on the Heights* (1959)

- Voice type: personal literary portrait of Brooklyn Heights.
- Important framing: Capote is **not** a Fulton Ferry passenger in this source. He is a shoreline/absence voice, living above a river crossing whose old daily service has vanished.
- Why it belongs: his chosen life in Brooklyn Heights supplies the glamorous, intimate view of the waterfront during the ferry’s “death interval.”
- Possible short opening to verify in a legitimate copy: “I live in Brooklyn. By choice.”
- Source metadata: [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/A_House_on_the_Heights.html?id=TlFaAAAAMAAJ)
- Background: [Brooklyn Magazine on Capote’s Brooklyn Heights](https://www.bkmag.com/2014/09/29/truman-capotes-brooklyn-heights/)

#### 3. Carol Sun, DUMBO oral history

- Voice type: retrospective oral history recorded in 2017.
- Scene: recalls walking through DUMBO in the 1970s while looking for artist-occupied buildings, moving there in 1979, marrying at Bargemusic, and witnessing successive waves of development.
- Why it belongs: personal life takes root beside an industrial, ferry-less waterfront; the abandoned district becomes home and later spectacle.
- Rights: the oral-history collection requires checking Brooklyn Collection/Center for Brooklyn History permission for public reuse. Link instead of embedding if permission is unavailable.
- Source: [DUMBO Neighborhood History Project oral histories, Center for Brooklyn History finding aid](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/cbh/2008_031/contents/aspace_c27b538144747de574d291b6050af4ca/)

#### 4. Crane Davis, DUMBO oral history

- Voice type: retrospective oral history.
- Scene: recalls organizing loft tenants and manufacturers in DUMBO in the 1970s and 1980s.
- Why it belongs: the waterfront is not empty during the ferry’s absence; it is occupied by work, art, housing, and political struggle.
- Rights: check the same collection’s public-reuse requirements.
- Source: [DUMBO Neighborhood History Project oral histories](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/cbh/2008_031/contents/aspace_c27b538144747de574d291b6050af4ca/)

## 7. Visual Direction

### Central visual metaphor

- Use an **aged beige paper texture** as the base.
- A fine **red thread** moves across the page, joining archive cards, dates, map locations, and Whitman motifs.
- The thread represents the East Asian cultural idea of people connected through fate, but it must not flatten distinct Chinese, Japanese, or other traditions into one generic claim.
- In the final essay or design note, describe it carefully as a visual metaphor for connection across separation and time. Add a researched cultural source if discussing the tradition historically.

### Style

- Warm beige, faded ink, charcoal, deep river blue, and restrained red.
- Archival typography paired with a highly readable body font.
- Gentle water-like movement, parallax, or thread animation only if it does not harm accessibility.
- Avoid making the site look like a supernatural fantasy or fortune-telling product.
- Preserve whitespace and legibility; the archive must remain the focus.

### Suggested micro-interactions

- Hover/focus causes the red thread to tighten between accounts sharing a motif.
- A **Mundane / Glamorous / Both** toggle changes which cards are emphasized.
- Scrolling through 1924 makes the thread break or disappear; it returns with renewed ferry service.
- Selecting a map point reveals every era attached to that place.
- Offer reduced-motion behavior through `prefers-reduced-motion`.

## 9. Research and Copyright Rules

1. **Never fabricate a voice.** No synthetic diary entries, composite riders, invented quotations, or fake reviews.
2. Every quotation must be checked against the original item or a trustworthy transcript.
3. Use no more text than necessary. For copyrighted reviews, books, essays, and forum posts, quote one brief phrase and paraphrase the rest.
4. Add the creator/display name, date, source title, direct link, and retrieval date to each online account.
5. Clearly label retrospective oral histories as memories recorded later, not documents created in the historical moment described.
6. Distinguish a direct ferry passenger from a waterfront observer. Capote, for example, represents Brooklyn Heights during the ferry’s absence rather than an active Fulton Ferry crossing.
7. Check image and audio rights item by item. A source being visible online does not make it free to republish.
8. If permission is unclear, show a citation and external link instead of embedding the item.
9. Do not expose personal data beyond what a contributor publicly included in the cited post.
10. Preserve uncertainty. Use labels such as “candidate source,” “date to verify,” or “permission needed” in the research interface until resolved.

## 12. Definition of Done

The website is ready when:

- the archive, not fictional characters, is the main experience;
- the three historical periods are understandable without instructor explanation;
- the 1924 closure and later return of ferry service are clearly represented;
- every public quotation is verified and cited;
